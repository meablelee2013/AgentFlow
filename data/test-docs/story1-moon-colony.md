# The Last Signal from Luna-7

Commander Elena Vasquez stared at the blinking red light on her console. Three days. That's how long it had been since the last transmission from Luna-7, the Moon's first permanent research colony. Three days of static, punctuated only by an automated distress beacon that repeated the same garbled message on a loop.

The year was 2087. Humanity had established five permanent settlements across the solar system, but Luna-7 was special. It wasn't just a research outpost; it was home to 247 scientists and their families. Among them was Elena's younger brother, Marco, an astrophysicist studying the Moon's seismic patterns.

"Ma'am, we're picking up something unusual," said Lieutenant Park, his voice tense. "It's not the beacon. It's... music."

Elena leaned closer to the speaker. Through the crackle of deep-space interference, she could hear it: a slow, haunting piano melody. It was Chopin's Nocturne in E-flat major — Marco's favorite piece. He played it every night before bed, a ritual from their childhood that he never abandoned.

"That's Marco," Elena whispered. "He's alive."

The room fell silent. Everyone knew what this meant. If Marco could play that piano, he was inside the colony's main habitat. And if he was inside, so were others. But why couldn't they communicate?

"Get me a direct channel to Artemis Command," Elena ordered. "Tell them we're going in."

The International Space Council had strict protocols for colony emergencies. Wait for the investigation team. Send drones first. Never risk an unscheduled rescue mission. But Elena had spent fifteen years following protocols, and she knew exactly how long an investigation took. By then, everyone on Luna-7 would be dead.

Her ship, the Artemis IV, wasn't designed for lunar landing. It was a transport vessel, built for cargo runs between Earth and the orbital stations. But Elena had a plan.

"The Moon's gravity is one-sixth of Earth's," she explained to her crew. "Artemis can't land, but we don't need to land. We just need to get close enough to deploy the emergency shuttle."

"And if the shuttle fails?" asked Park.

"Then we get creative."

As Artemis IV approached the Moon, the Chopin melody grew louder. Marco was playing it on repeat, probably using the colony's emergency broadcast system. Smart kid, Elena thought. A distress beacon would be ignored after the first day. But music? Music made people listen.

The lunar surface came into view, grey and cratered, with Luna-7's habitat dome glinting in the sunlight. Everything looked normal from the outside. The solar panels were deployed. The airlock seals were intact. There was no visible damage.

"Thermal scans show life signs," Park reported. "Multiple. At least a hundred people, clustered in the main habitat."

"Why can't they talk to us?" Elena muttered.

Then she saw it. A thin, almost invisible cloud of particles surrounding the colony's communication array. "Park, zoom in on the antenna array. What is that?"

Park adjusted the imaging. "It's... dust. Metallic dust. High concentration of iron and nickel."

"Regolith," Elena said. "Lunar soil. Something kicked it up into the array. It's blocking the signal."

"But the beacon gets through?"

"The beacon uses a lower frequency. It can penetrate the dust cloud. But voice and data? No chance."

The solution was simple but dangerous. Someone would need to go outside and clean the array. In a suit. On the Moon. With no way to communicate with the people inside.

"Prep the shuttle," Elena said. "I'm going down there."

"You can't be serious, Commander. The Council—"

"The Council is three days away. We're here. Now prep the shuttle."

The descent was the longest twelve minutes of Elena's life. The shuttle bucked and shuddered as it cut through the lunar dust cloud, visibility dropping to near zero. But the landing legs touched down smoothly on the pad beside Luna-7's main airlock.

Elena suited up and stepped onto the lunar surface. The dust crunched beneath her boots. Above her, Earth hung like a blue-and-white marble in the black sky. She walked toward the communication array, her boots leaving perfect prints in the ancient regolith.

The dust was thick on the antenna — a fine metallic coating that shimmered in her helmet light. She pulled out a brush from her suit's tool kit and began to clean, working methodically across each panel. Five minutes passed. Ten. Twenty.

Finally, the antenna was clear. She tapped her helmet radio. "Luna-7, this is Commander Elena Vasquez of Artemis IV. Do you copy?"

Static. Then:

"Elena?" Marco's voice cracked. "Is that really you?"

"Yeah, little brother. It's me."

The colony's lights flickered, and through the habitat's dome window, Elena could see people emerging from the central hall. They were thin, tired, but alive. A meteorite strike had shaken the colony three days earlier, disrupting communications and damaging the life support systems. The dust cloud was a side effect of the impact.

"I knew you'd come," Marco said. "I played your song."

"*Our* song," Elena corrected. "And it's still terrible."

She could hear him laugh through the radio — the first real sound from Luna-7 in three days.

That night, as Artemis IV ferried supplies down to the colony and engineers began repairs, Elena sat with her brother in the observatory dome. They watched Earth rise over the lunar horizon, a crescent of blue light against the darkness.

"You know," Marco said, "you violated about twenty Council protocols coming here."

"Twenty-three, actually." Elena smiled. "I counted."

The piano played softly in the corner, Chopin's notes filling the quiet dome. This time, it wasn't a distress signal. It was just music.
