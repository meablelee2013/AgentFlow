# The Garden Beneath the Waves

Dr. Yuki Tanaka had dedicated her life to a dream most people considered impossible: growing food on the ocean floor. After twelve years of research, three failed prototypes, and more grant rejections than she cared to count, she was finally about to prove them wrong.

The Nereus Dome sat sixty meters below the surface of the Philippine Sea, a geodesic structure of reinforced glass and titanium that looked like a giant soap bubble resting on the seabed. Inside, under carefully controlled conditions of temperature, humidity, and artificial sunlight, grew the world's first underwater farm.

Yuki descended through the access tube, the water pressure increasing with every meter. At the bottom, she stepped into the dome and removed her helmet. The air was warm and humid, heavy with the scent of soil and growing things. Rows of lettuce, tomatoes, and strawberries stretched before her, their leaves a vivid green under the LED lights.

"Good morning, Doctor," said KAI, the facility's AI assistant, its voice emanating from hidden speakers. "Soil moisture at optimal levels. Nutrient injection completed at 0400 hours. Tomato bed three is showing a two percent increase in growth rate."

"Thank you, KAI. Any anomalies?"

"Minor fluctuations in the desalination system. I have adjusted the filters accordingly."

Yuki walked through the rows, running her fingers over the leaves. It still amazed her that this was possible. The ocean covered seventy percent of the Earth's surface, yet humanity struggled to feed itself on land. By farming the seafloor, they could access near-infinite water, stable temperatures, and — most importantly — space. Enormous amounts of space.

The technology was deceptively simple. Reverse osmosis desalinated the seawater. Solar panels on the surface provided power. The dome's glass was treated with a special coating that amplified natural sunlight while blocking harmful UV radiation. And the seabed itself provided natural insulation, keeping the internal temperature at a constant twenty-two degrees Celsius regardless of surface weather.

But the real breakthrough was the soil. Yuki had spent six years developing a synthetic growth medium made from processed marine sediment, enriched with minerals extracted from deep-sea hydrothermal vents. The resulting soil was three times more fertile than the best terrestrial farmland.

"KAI, prepare the harvest protocol for the strawberries. We're shipping to the surface tomorrow."

"Protocol initiated, Doctor. Shall I notify the surface team?"

"Please."

The strawberries were the proof of concept. If they could be grown economically and sold at a competitive price, investors would flood in. And then Yuki could expand to staple crops. Rice. Wheat. Potatoes. Crops that could feed millions.

A shadow passed over the dome. Yuki looked up and saw a whale shark gliding overhead, its massive body casting a moving silhouette against the filtered sunlight. At twelve meters long, it was the largest fish in the ocean, yet completely harmless to humans. It seemed to pause for a moment, as if curious about the strange bubble of light and life on the seafloor. Then it flicked its tail and disappeared into the blue.

"You have a visitor," KAI observed.

"I saw."

"Incoming call from the surface. It's Mr. Chen from Oceanic Ventures."

Yuki sighed. Chen was their primary investor, and he called at least twice a day with increasingly anxious questions about profitability. She walked to the communication terminal and accepted the call.

"Dr. Tanaka! I've been trying to reach you for an hour. The board wants numbers. When can we expect the first commercial harvest?"

"Tomorrow, Mr. Chen. The strawberries are being harvested as we speak."

There was a pause. "Tomorrow? That's... that's excellent news! I'll inform the board immediately. And the projected yield?"

"Four hundred kilograms from the first section alone. Within acceptable variance of our model."

"Four hundred... Dr. Tanaka, do you realize what this means? We could undercut every strawberry producer in Southeast Asia!"

Yuki smiled. "That's the idea, Mr. Chen. But strawberries are just the beginning."

She ended the call and returned to her crops. The whale shark had moved on, but in its place, a school of tropical fish darted past the dome, their scales catching the light like a shower of gemstones. This was her office now: a glass bubble at the bottom of the sea, surrounded by the quiet majesty of the ocean.

By evening, the harvest was complete. Four hundred and twelve kilograms of perfectly ripe strawberries, each one grown without a single drop of fresh water from the surface. Yuki held one in her palm — red, fragrant, still warm from the artificial sun.

She bit into it. It was sweet. Incredibly sweet, with a depth of flavor that supermarket strawberries never achieved.

"KAI," she said, "make a note for the log."

"Ready, Doctor."

"Day one of commercial operation. The strawberries taste like victory."
